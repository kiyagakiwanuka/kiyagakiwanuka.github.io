Comparative Evaluation of Classical and Neo-Classical Trade Theories

Overview

Evaluated the Ricardian and Heckscher-Ohlin (H-O) models of international trade to determine their validity in explaining global trade patterns.

Context & question

Do the two foundational theories of trade - Ricardian comparative advantage and Heckscher-Ohlin factor endowments - actually explain the trade patterns we observe?

Trade theory underpins arguments about tariffs, industrial strategy and which sectors a country should expect to win or lose from openness. If the theories fail empirically, policy built on them inherits the error.

Key concepts

  • Ricardian model: Trade is driven by differences in labour productivity (technology). Each country gains by specialising in the good it produces at the lowest opportunity cost - comparative, not absolute, advantage.
  • Heckscher-Ohlin (H-O) model: Assumes shared technology and explains trade by factor endowments: a country exports goods that use its relatively abundant factor intensively (capital-rich countries export capital-intensive goods).
  • Leontief Paradox: Wassily Leontief's 1953 input-output study found that US exports were less capital-intensive than its import substitutes - the opposite of what H-O predicted for the world's most capital-abundant economy. Proposed explanations include differences in labour skill (human capital), natural resources and trade barriers.
  • Heckscher-Ohlin-Vanek (H-O-V): Generalises H-O to many goods and factors by predicting the factor content of trade: countries should be net exporters of the services of their abundant factors. Empirical tests found weak fit ('missing trade') until technology differences across countries were allowed.
  • Theory vs evidence: A recurring lesson: Ricardian productivity differences and H-O endowments each explain part of the picture, and modern work combines them.

Methodology & process

  1. Set out the assumptions and predictions of each model side by side.
  2. Reviewed the empirical literature, starting with the Leontief Paradox and the explanations offered for it.
  3. Assessed how H-O-V reframes the test around the factor content of trade.
  4. Weighed where each theory holds and where it breaks down, and concluded on their explanatory validity.

Why it matters

Demonstrates a strong grasp of macroeconomic foundations and the ability to critically assess theoretical frameworks.

Useful for policy analysis or market research where identifying the gap between theory and actual market behavior is essential.

Research and policy roles regularly require judging whether a neat model survives contact with data. This project is a structured example of testing theory against evidence and stating the gap clearly.
