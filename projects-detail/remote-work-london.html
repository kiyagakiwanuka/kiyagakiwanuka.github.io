Overview

Investigated the factors influencing remote work, specifically looking at the impact of housing conditions and education levels.

Context & question

What determines who can work from home in Greater London?

Remote work is often discussed as a question of job type or individual skill. This project tested a less visible constraint: whether people have the physical space at home to work at all.

Key concepts

  • Coefficient interpretation: In a regression, a coefficient measures the expected change in the outcome for a one-unit change in an explanatory variable, holding other factors constant; interpreting it correctly (units, direction, significance) is what turns a model into a usable statement.
  • Overcrowding as a constraint: Housing conditions shape whether a quiet, dedicated workspace exists, so they can limit remote working independently of a worker's occupation or qualifications.
  • Human capital: Education raises the likelihood of holding knowledge-intensive roles, which are the roles most easily performed away from a workplace.

Methodology & process

  1. Built an econometric model of the likelihood of working from home.
  2. Interpreted coefficients on home overcrowding and education.

Data & tools

econometric modeling

Findings & outcomes

  • For every 1% increase in home overcrowding, the likelihood of working from home decreased by 1.59%.
  • Education was identified as a key facilitator of flexible roles.
  • The physical home environment matters as much as individual skills, with implications for HR strategy, urban planning and social policy.

Why it matters

Provides evidence-based insights into the modern labor market.

Employers and public bodies planning hybrid working, office space or housing policy need evidence on who can actually work remotely. Translating a coefficient into a sentence a decision-maker can act on is a core analyst skill.
