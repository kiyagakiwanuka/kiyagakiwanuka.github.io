Political Economy of Climate Change: Climate Justice in Bangladesh and Tuvalu

OverviewContextConceptsMethodologyWhy it mattersSkills gained

Overview

Analyzed climate justice and the economic responsibilities of developed nations toward developing ones.

Context & question

What do developed nations owe developing nations that bear climate damage they did little to cause?

Key concepts

  • Climate externality: Emissions impose costs on others who are not party to the decision; climate change is a global negative externality, with costs borne disproportionately by low-emitting countries.
  • Climate justice: Frames responsibility in terms of historical emissions and capacity to pay, not only current emissions.
  • Loss and damage: Covers climate harms that adaptation cannot prevent. A dedicated fund was agreed at COP27 in 2022, but scale, contributors and eligibility remain contested.
  • Climate-induced migration: Displacement from rising seas, flooding and extreme weather carries long-term economic and social costs and has weak legal protection.

Methodology & process

  1. Compared Bangladesh (densely populated, flood-exposed) and Tuvalu (a low-lying island state facing existential sea-level risk) as contrasting cases.
  2. Evaluated the loss and damage framework against the principles of climate justice.
  3. Examined the political barriers to financing it and the long-run costs of climate-induced migration.

Why it matters

Focuses on the ethical and environmental dimensions of economics.

It builds an understanding of economic externalities (how actions of one group harm others) which is highly valuable for ESG consulting and international development roles.

ESG, sustainable finance and international development roles require reasoning about externalities and responsibility, not just emissions numbers. This project frames climate as an economic allocation problem.
